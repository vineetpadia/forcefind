Typical Operating Characteristics





Application Information


 This sensor is a compact silicon device designed to measure applied forces from 0 N to 10 N with milli-newton-level resolution. The achievable
 resolution depends on the bit depth and performance of the external analog-to-digital converter (ADC) used in the system.
 Individual sensor or sensor arrays may be mounted beneath a touch surface to enable force-based human–machine interfaces (HMI). When
 appropriately integrated, the sensors support a range of use cases including discrete force-sensitive buttons, trigger mechanisms, stylus
 detection, and single-axis robotic or control inputs.

System Stack-up

 In button‑type implementations, the sensor is actuated through a layer of silicone rubber positioned beneath the device’s external surface
(made by any material). The rubber layer protects the sensor and helps absorb manufacturing tolerances commonly encountered in display,
 button, or button-under-surface applications. With a properly designed stack-up, the sensor can reliably detect applied forces from a few grams
up to 1000 grams or more. For mechanical integration, a 2 mil (50 µm) double-sided adhesive is recommended for bonding the silicone rubber to
 the underlying stack-up.





                        Recommended Sensor Stack-up With Spacer (Requires Contact on Both Sides)

A 70 Shore A adhesive-backed silicone rubber—for example, Ames Rubber Manufacturing 0.015 SIL GRY—is recommended to accommodate
 typical vertical tolerances. To accommodate a mechanical tolerance of ±100 µm, a 400 µm-thick rubber layer is used, providing a nominal
 compression of approximately 110 µm during actuation (see below table). To absorb larger mechanical tolerances, contact Semtech for further

Electrical Design, Layout, Placement and Handling

Electrical Design Considerations


 The sensor is a sensitive analog device. All routing, bypassing, and component placement practices described in this section are intended to
 preserve signal integrity, minimize noise coupling, and ensure reliable operation across the operating temperature and supply-voltage range.
    •   Power all analog circuitry from a low-noise supply source, preferably a dedicated on-board LDO.
    •   Match the SPOS and SNEG trace lengths and minimize parasitic asymmetry between the sensor and the AFE differential inputs.
    •   At AFE side, two bypass capacitors are required, 2.2 µF & 1 nF, to reduce noise on the power supply. Use higher value bypass capacitors
          for noisy power supplies. Ensure that both capacitors are placed close to the AFE VDD pin.
    •   Connect sensor SE pin via a 0-ohm resistor directly to the grounded terminal of the bypass capacitors next to the AFE. The 0-ohm resistor
        and the bypass capacitors should be ≤3 mm away from the AFE.
    •   Add a 1 nF differential filter capacitor (highlighted in the table below) between SPOS and SNEG and place the differential capacitor close
         to the differential inputs of the AFE.

 The differential capacitor value affects both sensor power-on settling time and the analog noise-filter cut-off frequency. After DRV from AFE is
 applied to the sensor, the AFE must wait (PRECHARGE register should be programmed to accommodate this external RC time constant) for the
 sensor to settle before sampling. Sensor power-on settling time and noise-filter cut-off frequency as a function of capacitor value are tabulated
 in the table below.

    •   Do not pick up the device with metal tweezers. Use a vacuum pickup tool.
    •   Do not drop MEMS devices
         o   Devices are specified for up to 10,000 g shock absorption
         o   10,000 g can cause damage to the moving micromechanical structures
    •   Do not “snap” panelized, assembled PCBs.
         o   Avoid damage to the device due to potential shock of > 10,000 g
    •   Do not ultrasonic clean the MEMS

SMT Pick-and-Place Guidelines

Nozzle Considerations


SMT assembly machines typically employ a vacuum nozzle for Pick & Place (P&P) operations. Not all equipment is the same, but there are some
 general guidelines to follow, for optimal performance/yield.
    •    Select a vacuum nozzle only.
         o  DO NOT use mechanical grippers or collets that contact the edges/sides of a WLCSP.
         o  DO NOT handle WLCSP parts with metal tweezers.
    •    Select softer nozzle materials where possible.
         o   Nozzle materials can vary (metal, ceramic, plastic, rubber), and certain equipment requires certain nozzle materials. Select softer
                 material to avoid potential damage from mechanical shocks.
    •    Select an appropriate nozzle tip shape.
         o   Nozzle tip shape can affect the amount of pressure applied to a single point on a WLCSP part.
         o   Nozzle tips should be circular, square/rectangular, and preferably with a vertical separator.
         o   The nozzle tip surface should be a single plane; do not use a nozzle with a protruded edge.





                                                 Acceptable Vs NOT acceptable Tip Shapes

    •    Select an appropriate nozzle tip size, for the part being placed.
         o   The largest tip dimension should be smaller than the size of the WLCSP part.
         o   The tip should account for the tolerance of the pick accuracy, so that it’s not possible for the nozzle tip to contact the edge or the
                corner of a part.
         o   The tip size should be as large as possible, while satisfying the above 2 points.

Pick and Place Operation


The P&P operation consists of two key steps: picking a part out of the carrier tape pocket, and placement of that part onto a circuit board. Both
 operations are performed with some alignment tolerance: nozzle-to-part tolerance during the pick, and part-to-board alignment during the
placement. Additionally, the placement should occur with as little force as possible, to press the die into place on the board.

WLCSP parts are shipped to customers in standard carrier tape & reel packaging. Parts sit in a pocket that is slightly larger than the size of the
 part. A part can move laterally inside the pocket, typically by less than 0.1 mm, but this varies by product.
    •      If available, the P&P equipment should use a vision detection system to align the P&P nozzle to the center of the die, for every part picked.
    •      If a vision alignment system isn’t available, the nozzle size selection should account for the positional tolerance of the WLCSP part in the
           carrier tape pocket.

The die placement operation should be performed with a placement accuracy of ≤0.1 mm; placement accuracy of ≤0.05 mm is typical with
modern P&P equipment. The placement force shall be the minimum required to bring the WLCSP solder bumps into contact with the solder
 paste on the board.
A contactless vacuum pickup is strongly preferred; the vacuum force alone should lift the part with only incidental surface contact to the die top.
    •   Use air-ejection placement in preference to contact placement. A typical air-ejection pressure is 150 mbar.
    •      If air placement isn’t available, use a contact mode for the part placement. Ensure that both the pick and placement force is not larger
         than 2 N (~200 gf). Placement forces should be verified periodically using a calibrated load cell, to ensure parts aren’t placed with a force
         over this specification.

GLOSSARY (Alphabetical Order)


 Bridge Resistance – Resistance expressed in kΩ when measured between the positive and the negative output terminals of the sensor.

CDM (Charge Device Model) – CDM is a model for characterizing the susceptibility of an electronic device to damage from electrostatic discharge
 (ESD). CDM is commonly used for semiconductor devices and has been standardized in JEDEC with JESD22-C101E.

 Full-Scale Range (FSR) – Full-Scale Range or FSR is the maximum operational range of the sensor and is measured in N.

HBM (Human Body Model) – HBM is the most used model for characterizing the susceptibility of an electronic device to damage from
 electrostatic discharge (ESD). The model simulates an ESD discharge that may occur when a human contacts an electronic device.

 Hysteresis – Hysteresis is the maximum deviation, expressed as a percentage of full-scale range (%FSR), between sensor output on increasing
 force and sensor output on decreasing force.

 Linearity – Linearity is the deviation of sensor output from a perfect linear response. Actual sensor data is “aligned” to the perfect linear
response with a best straight-line fit approach. Linearity is unit-less.

Noise – Sensor noise is defined as the random deviation of the sensor’s output signal, which varies in time and is measured in nV/rt-Hz.

 Sensitivity – This parameter defines how much the sensor output changes with applied force. Sensitivity is measured in mV/ N.

ZFO (Zero-Force Offset) – ZFO is the natural sensor offset in mV with no force (0 N) applied to the sensor.
